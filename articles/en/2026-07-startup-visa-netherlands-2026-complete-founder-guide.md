# Startup Visa Netherlands (2026): The Complete Founder Guide

> Thinking about moving your startup to the Netherlands? This guide explains how the Dutch Startup Visa really works, what RVO actually evaluates, the most common reasons applications fail, and how to prepare before investing months into the process.

**Last updated:** July 2026

---

# Who this guide is for

This guide is written for founders who:

- already have a startup or MVP;
- are planning to build a company in the Netherlands;
- have been approached by a Dutch facilitator;
- have already received a rejection;
- want to understand whether the Startup Visa is the right route before spending time and money.

It is not intended for freelancers, digital nomads or people simply looking for residence in Europe. The Dutch Startup Visa is an innovation programme, not an immigration shortcut.

---

# Before you start: should you even apply?

Many founders mistakenly believe that only deep-tech startups or groundbreaking scientific inventions qualify for the Dutch Startup Visa.

In reality, almost any business idea can become a Startup Visa candidate if it demonstrates genuine innovation, has long-term growth potential and can create value for the Dutch economy.

Innovation does not necessarily mean inventing a new technology. It can be an innovative product, service, business model, production method, market approach or combination of existing technologies that solves a problem in a new way.

Even an existing business can qualify if its Dutch expansion introduces an innovative element or creates opportunities that did not previously exist in the Netherlands.

The challenge is that many founders either underestimate the innovative aspects of their business or fail to present them convincingly.

This is where expert guidance often makes the biggest difference.

Many founders spend months preparing documents before asking the most important question:

**Is the Startup Visa actually the right route for my business?**

In practice, the answer is often "no".

Common examples include:

- agencies;
- software development outsourcing;
- marketing companies;
- standard e-commerce stores;
- consulting businesses without a scalable product.

These businesses may be profitable, but profitability alone does not make them innovative in the eyes of the Dutch authorities.

On the other hand, some founders dismiss themselves too early, while in reality their business contains innovative elements that simply have not yet been recognised or positioned correctly.

Sometimes another residence permit is objectively a better solution.

Sometimes a Startup Visa becomes the strongest option after restructuring the case.

Understanding this before applying can save months of work, thousands of euros and significant frustration.

---

# What is the Dutch Startup Visa?

The Startup Visa allows founders from outside the EU/EEA to spend one year in the Netherlands building an innovative company under the guidance of an approved Dutch facilitator.

The visa itself is temporary.

The objective is to transition to a residence permit as a self-employed entrepreneur before the first year ends.

This distinction is important.

The Startup Visa is not the final goal.

It is the first step.

---

# How the process actually works

Many websites simplify the process to:

**Founder → Facilitator → IND → Visa**

Reality is far more complicated.

A typical application includes:

1. Initial assessment of the startup.
2. Finding a suitable facilitator.
3. Preparing the cooperation agreement.
4. Innovation assessment.
5. Market validation.
6. Business planning.
7. IND submission.
8. RVO advice.
9. IND decision.
10. Arrival in the Netherlands.
11. Product development.
12. Transition to self-employed residence.

And these are only the formal administrative and regulatory milestones.

They do not include product development, customer discovery, fundraising, hiring, relocation, market validation, negotiations with facilitators or the dozens of strategic decisions founders face while building a company in a new country.

Each stage can fail independently.

---

# The biggest misconception

Many founders believe:

> "If I find a facilitator, I will receive the visa."

This is incorrect.

A facilitator cannot approve an application.

Nor can a lawyer.

Nor can an immigration consultant.

The final decision belongs to IND after receiving advice from RVO.

A strong facilitator can significantly strengthen your chances.

However, no facilitator can guarantee approval.

---

# What RVO actually looks for

This is where many applications fail.

The question is not:

> "Is this a good business?"

The question is much closer to:

> "Is this startup sufficiently innovative to justify this specific residence route?"

Innovation is evaluated through multiple factors, including:

- novelty;
- technology;
- market differentiation;
- scalability;
- business model;
- execution plan;
- founder capability.

A startup does not need to invent new technology.

However, it should demonstrate meaningful innovation compared with existing market solutions.

---

# The three questions I ask every founder

Before discussing documents, I usually ask three questions.

## 1. Why can't this company be launched anywhere else?

If the answer is weak, innovation is usually weak.

## 2. Why is the Netherlands the right place?

Many founders never answer this convincingly.

A strong answer usually connects the startup with the Dutch ecosystem, talent, market access, research environment, industry clusters or strategic partnerships.

## 3. Why now?

Many founders believe they already know whether they qualify.

In practice, they often underestimate the innovative aspects of their business or overlook opportunities that could make their case significantly stronger.

Sometimes a traditional business already contains an innovative product, technology or process that simply has never been positioned correctly.

Sometimes the Startup Visa is not the best solution at all, and another immigration route is faster, cheaper and more appropriate.

The only reliable way to know is to assess the business before investing months into an application.

---

# Why applications are rejected

Most refusals are not caused by paperwork.

They are caused by positioning.

Typical reasons include:

- weak innovation;
- generic business plans;
- unclear product-market fit;
- poor explanation of competitive advantage;
- facilitator mismatch;
- insufficient evidence;
- unrealistic execution roadmap.

Another common reason is that founders prepare the application themselves.

Self-prepared applications are frequently rejected not because the business lacks potential, but because the innovation, commercial value and relevance for the Netherlands are not communicated clearly enough.

A good startup can still become a weak application.

Often the founder has a genuinely promising company.

The application simply fails to communicate its strengths.
